*Amid market records and geopolitical shifts, global demand for gold bars keeps climbing. At the centre of this financial and industrial architecture sits Switzerland, and in particular a strip of just a few kilometres in the Mendrisiotto, home to more than half of the world's refining capacity.*

On 29 January gold hit an all-time high, just under 5,600 dollars an ounce. The next day it lost 9.5%, its worst session since 1983, when the White House named the new chair of the Federal Reserve. By the end of October it was trading about a quarter below its peak. The correction did not change the underlying picture: in 2025 global demand had passed 5,000 tonnes for the first time, driven by investors and by central bank purchases that stayed very high.

Behind the prices quoted in London and New York, however, lies a highly concentrated physical infrastructure. All the gold ever mined, about 220,000 tonnes, would fit in a cube 22 metres on each side. A significant share of what is refined each year comes out of a few kilometres of land between Balerna, Castel San Pietro and Mendrisio.

## A lead worth measuring carefully

It is often said that Switzerland refines 70% of the world's gold. The figure was given in 2012 by an industry spokesperson and has been repeated ever since with little checking. Recent estimates are more sober. According to the State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO), in 2023 the country produced about a third of the world's refined gold; the industry association puts it at 35–40%.

The lead is still clear. Five Swiss refineries are accredited by the London Bullion Market Association (LBMA), the body that sets standards for the wholesale market: two in the Canton of Neuchâtel and three in Ticino.

## From doré to bar

Refineries take in three kinds of material: doré, a crude alloy cast directly at the mine; jewellery and scrap for recycling; and less pure bars to be remelted. What comes out are standard products. The benchmark is the Good Delivery bar, about 400 troy ounces, just over 12 kilos, with a minimum purity of 99.5%. Bars for private buyers and industry reach 999.9 parts per thousand.

The added value is more a matter of trust than of chemistry. The hallmark of an accredited refinery guarantees the metal's weight and fineness, so it can circulate anywhere without being tested again: this is what turns a raw material into a liquid asset. The business therefore lives on volume and reputation rather than margins. To refine a kilo of gold and cast it into a bar, explains the president of the Swiss industry association, a refinery often earns less than one part per thousand of its value. Small minted products for the watch industry pay far better.

The customers reflect gold's dual nature, as both metal and money. There are mining groups that hand over their output as they would to a service provider. There are specialised banks and ETF issuers that buy bars on behalf of investors, and central banks that usually buy them through intermediaries. Finally, there are jewellers, watchmakers and the electronics industry, which need alloys and semi-finished products. In 2025 investment absorbed more than four tonnes of gold in ten, jewellery about three, and central banks fewer than two.

## The format bridge

Switzerland's role shows most clearly in times of stress. The London market, whose vaults hold about 9,500 tonnes of gold, works with 400-ounce bars. New York's Comex, by contrast, accepts one-kilo or 100-ounce bars. That is why London metal, before crossing the Atlantic, is usually recast in Switzerland. The same applies to Asia's physical markets, which are used to the one-kilo format.

In early 2025, fear of US tariffs opened a gap between prices in New York and London. In January Swiss exports to the United States set a monthly record, while the Mendrisiotto refineries ran around the clock. In August US customs listed one-kilo bars among the goods subject to the 39% tariff on Swiss products. The White House exempted gold a few days later, but in the meantime shipments to the United States had almost stopped. The later agreement, which cut tariffs to 15%, leaves refined gold out.

## Three towns, three profiles

The concentration in Ticino has historical roots. In the 1970s and 1980s Italy was the world's largest jewellery producer and sourced refined gold just across the border. On top of the proximity to the goldsmith districts came political stability and financial services, as well as a federal law that since 1933 has required a licence to melt precious metals. The result is a district where three world-scale operators work a few kilometres from one another, and from USI's Mendrisio campus.

In **Balerna**, what is considered the world's largest refinery has operated since 1961. Since 2015 it has belonged to an Indian group, the world's largest maker of gold jewellery. Its stated capacity, 1,600 tonnes of gold a year, equals almost half of global mine production.

In **Castel San Pietro**, a plant opened in 1977 is now part of a Geneva-based precious metals trading group and is best known for minted bars aimed at private buyers.

**Mendrisio** is home to the canton's oldest refinery, founded in Chiasso in 1951 and wholly owned by a German industrial group since 2017. In 2022 it acquired a Ticino-based supplier to the watch and jewellery industry.

## What stays in Ticino

The 70% figure that does hold up is a different one: in 2025 Ticino generated almost 70% of Switzerland's metal exports. According to the Federal Office for Customs and the cantonal statistics office, the canton exported metals, overwhelmingly precious ones, worth 118 billion francs. That is more than nineteen francs in every twenty of Ticino's total exports, and over three times the cantonal GDP.

These figures measure a flow, not wealth. Almost all the metal comes from abroad, and its declared value tracks the price, which rose by about half over the year. What stays locally is the added value, made up of wages, services and taxes, which a preliminary estimate puts at over 100 million francs. Direct jobs exceed 1,200. How much the wider supply chain is worth is the subject of an ongoing study by the industry association.

## The question of origin

Swiss customs record the country where the gold was last processed, not where it was mined. For years non-governmental organisations have called for more transparency on provenance. In 2025 more than a third of Ticino's imports came from the United Arab Emirates. The industry association has advised its members not to buy metal from Dubai, considering it hard to guarantee that it is fully legal; not all of the canton's refineries follow this advice.

The gold price will keep depending on interest rates, the dollar and geopolitics, variables beyond Ticino's control. The district's resilience depends instead on something within its reach: the ability to document, bar by bar, where the metal comes from.
